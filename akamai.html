Approach & workflow

My problem-solving process, the workflow this prototype supports, how priority is scored, and the alternatives I dismissed.

Who I designed for

A security analyst who is reviewing one spec against live traffic. They are security-savvy but usually not the engineer who owns the API, so their job is to separate real risk from noise and hand each issue to the right place. They think in risk and action, not in raw diffs.

My approach, in two paragraphs

Security reviewers don’t want a diff; they want a verdict. So the product opens on a risk-ranked triage queue instead of a raw list of differences. Every difference becomes an Issue with a transparent Priority Score built from the five signals the team said they care about — issue type, area sensitivity (Payments and Users weighted highest), traffic volume, recency, and exposure (unauthenticated requests plus sensitive data like PII or card numbers). The result: an unauthenticated shadow refund endpoint in Payments floats to the top, while an unused query parameter on a public catalog route sinks to the bottom. Faceted filters and three grouping modes (ranked, by area, by recommended action) let a reviewer slice the same data for their context — “show me only Payments” or “show me everything that needs a developer” — without ever losing the ranking.

The second half of the job is deciding what to do. Each issue carries a single recommended action — Investigate, Developer follow-up, Update spec, or No action — derived from the same signals, so the queue doubles as a worklist. Opening an issue reveals a “Why it matters” panel: plain-language risk, the spec-vs-traffic diff, and real sampled requests as evidence, plus a breakdown of exactly why the score is what it is. One-click actions then route the issue to the right destination — open an investigation, file a developer ticket, accept the change into the spec, or acknowledge it. The whole design exists to move a reviewer from “here are hundreds of differences” to “here are the few that matter, and here’s the button that resolves each” in minutes.

How priority is scored

A deliberately simple, explainable 0–100 model — reviewers can trust a number they can reason about. Each issue’s detail panel shows this exact breakdown.

▲ Issue type · ≤30

Shadow API > undocumented param > mismatch > zombie > unused. The type that most often signals real risk weighs most.

⬢ Area sensitivity · ≤20

Payments (×1.0) → Users (×0.9) → Orders (×0.6) → Catalog (×0.35), per the brief’s emphasis on sensitive areas.

📈 Traffic · ≤25

Log-scaled request volume — a problem hit thousands of times matters more than one hit twice.

⏱ Recency · ≤10

Seen in the last 6h scores highest. Recent activity is more actionable than something last seen days ago.

🔓 Exposure · ≤15

Unauthenticated traffic and detected sensitive data (PAN, credentials, PII) add the final, sharpest weight.

Alternatives I dismissed

✕ Raw spec-vs-traffic diff as the home screen

Makes the human compute risk for every line. Doesn’t scale past a few dozen diffs and buries the shadow APIs that matter most.

✕ Sort purely by traffic volume

A busy-but-benign param change would outrank a low-volume unauthenticated Payments shadow API. Volume is one signal, not the ranking.

✕ Endpoint graph / network visualization

Demos well, decides poorly. For triage, a scannable ranked table beats a force-directed graph every time.

✕ Auto-update the spec from traffic

Security reviewers must stay in control. The system recommends; the human decides. Trust is the product.

Assumptions I made

✓ This is the review layer, not discovery

The platform already parses the spec, captures traffic, classifies sensitive data, and computes the diff. I design the triage on top.

✓ Areas are a known grouping

Orders/Users/Catalog/Payments come from spec tags or path prefixes, with sensitivity ranking given by the brief.

✓ Actions integrate with existing tooling

“File ticket” reaches Jira/ServiceNow; “Update spec” opens a PR against the spec repo. Mocked here as confirmations.

✓ Scope: one reviewer, one run

I focused on a single 7-day run. Trends across runs and team assignment are noted but not built out in this timebox.

What I’d explore next

Run-over-run trend (“is drift getting better or worse?”), bulk triage for clusters of related shadow endpoints, owner auto-routing from a service catalog, and a confidence indicator on sensitive-data detection so reviewers know how much to trust the exposure signal.
